You are given the script of a short film -- its spoken lines in order, each with
the seconds it runs -- and the reference sheets that exist for it: its
characters, and its locations. Break the script into scenes and describe the
frame each scene opens on and the frame it ends on.

A **scene** is a run of consecutive lines that happen in one place, in one
continuous stretch of time. Keep the lines in the order they are given and use
every one of them exactly once: a scene is a span, never a selection. Prefer
three to eight scenes for a script of any length -- one scene per line is a list
of shots, and two scenes for twenty lines says nothing about how the film is
built.

For each scene, in order:

- **lines** -- the numbers of the script lines it covers, consecutive and
  ascending. Every line number you were given appears in exactly one scene.
- **title** -- three or four words naming the moment, as a person would say it
  out loud. "Arriving at the dock", "the argument". Not "Scene 2".
- **location** -- the number of the location sheet this scene happens in, or
  null when none of them is the place. Reuse a location across scenes when the
  story stays there.
- **characters** -- the numbers of the character sheets who are on screen in
  this scene. Usually all of them in a film with one character.
- **opening** -- the first frame of the scene, as a description of one still
  image. Who is in it, where they are, what they are doing at the instant the
  scene begins, how the camera is placed, and the light.
- **closing** -- the last frame of the same scene, described the same way. It is
  the same place, the same people and the same look, later: what has changed is
  the action, the position and the framing, because time has passed and the
  lines have been said.

What makes this worth looking at is whether the frames read as a story when they
are put one under another. So:

- **The opening and the closing of one scene must differ visibly.** Two near
  identical frames say the scene did not happen. Move somebody, change the
  framing, let the light or the weather turn.
- **The closing of one scene and the opening of the next must cut together.**
  Different framing, a changed place or a changed distance -- a cut, not a
  nudge. Keep screen direction and geography consistent across the join so the
  two frames read as one film.
- **Vary the shot sizes across the film.** An establishing wide, a medium, a
  close view of a face or a detail. Six frames of the same distance is a
  contact sheet, not a storyboard.

Describe only what is in the frame. No camera jargon standing in for a picture,
no captions, no shot numbers, no titles, no speech bubbles, and never the
dialogue as printed text. Do not describe the medium or the style: the frames
are generated from the reference sheets and inherit the film's own look.
